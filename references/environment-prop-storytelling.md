# Environment prop storytelling

## Problem and design reasoning

Individual props may look plausible while their arrangement implies an accidental or contradictory story. This guide focuses on designing and maintaining a small evidence cluster, such as a workbench, interrupted meal, or abandoned checkpoint. [Environmental storytelling](environmental-storytelling.md) covers the broader narrative structure and discovery order.

Harvey Smith and Matthias Worch's GDC 2010 presentation analyzes *BioShock*'s disrupted New Year's party, where decorations and other traces imply the state of Rapture before its collapse. They also describe familiar objects as cues to a place's function and possible resources. Fullbright's *Gone Home* developer talk describes designing the experience of inhabiting a house and story. These establish the value of contextual relationships. The evidence-cluster workflow below is a recommended application. [Smith and Worch, What Happened Here?, GDC 2010, slides 7–14](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc10/slides/Smith_Harvey_WhatHappenedHereWeb_Notes.pdf) · [Kate Craig and Steve Gaynor, The Level Design of Gone Home, GDC 2015](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1022112/Level-Design-in-a-Day)

## Practical techniques

### Define the cluster's ordinary use and disruption

Describe who used the objects, what they were doing, and what changed. Choose a few relationships that support that account, such as a tool beside unfinished work or a chair moved from a table. Keep incidental wear compatible with those relationships.

**Check** show the prop cluster without narration and ask unfamiliar viewers what activity took place and what seems unusual. Record which objects support each interpretation. Revise misleading relationships before adding more clutter.

### Author a hierarchy of evidence

Give the central relationship a readable silhouette or composition from the expected approach. Use secondary details to refine it at inspection distance. Match written labels and small surface detail to the actual gameplay camera and display scale.

**Check** inspect the cluster from the approach, at interaction distance, and on the smallest supported display. Compare which essential fact survives each view. Confirm an optional close-up detail is not the sole source of required comprehension.

### Make prop interaction preserve the intended account

Decide which items can move, break, disappear, or return after reload. If an important clue is portable, retain a record of discovery or another context cue. Give shared prefab variations clear ownership so one artist's change does not silently alter unrelated scenes.

**Check** move or destroy the most important prop where supported, then revisit and reload. Verify the scene still communicates any required fact and that the player's record agrees with their interaction history.

### Validate narrative dressing against gameplay

Review the cluster for accidental affordances, blocked paths, and conflict with combat or interaction readability. An object that looks collectible should follow the game's visual vocabulary. Make deliberate exceptions recognizable in play.

**Check** traverse and interact with the dressed space at ordinary speed. Log mistaken pickups, collisions, and missed gameplay cues. Repeat during the nearby encounter and under shipping lighting to test whether the story detail competes with required actions.
