# Mobile store delivery

## Problem and design reasoning

A mobile build can pass a sideloaded smoke test and still fail on first launch after a store install. Store packaging can select variants, defer content, and update assets on a schedule different from the executable. Large updates can also require enough temporary space to prevent existing players from patching. Make time to first playable, installed storage, and update reliability measurable release criteria.

CD Projekt RED identified storage pressure and full reinstalls during updates as a source of player frustration in *GWENT: The Witcher Card Game*. Its lead producer described delta patching and support for both in-game and store downloads as reasons for adopting Play Asset Delivery. Google reports that the team moved from on-demand packs to fast-follow packs to begin the download before first launch. The transferable inference is to choose delivery timing according to when a player needs the content. [Google Play case study and CD Projekt RED interview](https://google.play/business/case-studies/cdpr/)

## Shipped examples and conference evidence

- **Asphalt 8, Asphalt Xtreme, and Minion Rush.** Google documented Gameloft’s integration of Play Asset Delivery. The studio had several shipped games using the same store delivery technology, which gives an example of moving content delivery into the platform rather than duplicating the integration for each title. The source does not establish that each title used the same asset split. [Android Developers’ Play Asset Delivery launch announcement](https://android-developers.googleblog.com/2020/06/introducing-google-play-asset-delivery.html)
- **GWENT: The Witcher Card Game.** The Android game shipped with a large content footprint. The subsequent switch to fast-follow delivery let background downloads start before the player opened the game. Treat Google’s reported patch-size improvement as a case result and measure your own release. [Google Play case study and CD Projekt RED interview](https://google.play/business/case-studies/cdpr/)
- **Apple platform delivery.** Apple’s engineers explain essential, prefetch, and on-demand policies and demonstrate a tutorial-first split. For Apple-hosted packs they also explain that older binaries in a distribution context can be served the latest published pack. Those behaviors require separate readiness and compatibility checks. [Discover Apple-Hosted Background Assets, WWDC25](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2025/325/)

## Practical techniques and checks

1. **Make first playable a defined milestone.** Trace dependencies from launch to the first meaningful interaction. Assign that minimum set to the delivery tier that guarantees availability at the intended point. Keep subsequent areas, optional voices, and high-resolution content deferrable where their absence has a clear presentation. **Check** install from a real store test track on a clean device, open immediately with background packs pending, and measure elapsed install-to-play time. Verify that menus and onboarding behave as designed while later content downloads.
2. **Treat pack readiness as a recoverable state.** Model pending, downloading, available, and failed states. Resolve the asset through the platform after a foreground transition or restart. Gate entry to a dependent activity when required content is absent and offer retry or return to available play. **Check** interrupt a download with airplane mode and app termination, then retry under constrained free space. Verify accurate progress, useful messaging, and no partial content activation.
3. **Validate delivery variants and version compatibility.** Give packs a content version and declare the formats each supported build can load. Verify the store delivered the expected device variant. When pack versions can advance independently, stage compatible content before retiring an older format. **Check** install both a current and previous binary from the intended testing channels, then publish a pack-only update. Confirm both receive compatible content or an explicit, recoverable compatibility response.
4. **Measure upgrades on available disk space.** Track base download size, first-play asset size, update transfer, peak disk use during installation, and final footprint. Include retained caches and temporary copies in the storage test. **Check** upgrade from an active older build with player data and just above the minimum free-space budget. Measure the actual transfer and peak occupancy, then launch and verify saves, installed packs, and offline behavior.
